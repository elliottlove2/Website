Reflections, Rotations, and Spinors

This post is about the math of rotations. The 2D warmup interactively demonstrates how composing an even number of reflections creates rotations and how the angle doubling leads to spin. The 3D section extends this, along with a detour on the Bloch sphere and single qubit operations. After this I include a little bit of the math behind this. This includes how these animations were generated, and hopefully the general theory makes more sense after playing with the demos.

2D

Reflections in 2D provide a simple starting point for this post. In two dimensions, a mirror is a line called the \(\textit{axis of reflection}\). An object on one side of the axis gets flipped to the other side. But how do we go from reflections to rotations? In the simulation below, you can see how two reflections combine to form a single rotation.

Rotating with mirrors

The mirrors are described by their normal vectors: arrows at right angles to the mirrors. Move the mirrors around, and notice how changing the angle between them changes the rotation. Two things to look out for: first, when the normal vectors are flipped, the mirrors stay the same. Second, the rotation angle is twice the angle between the mirrors.

Loops of Rotations

Two reflections rotate the cat through twice the angle between the mirrors. Here, \(u\) stays fixed while \(w\), the other mirror’s normal arrow, turns.

After the cat travels around \(2\pi\) radians, the mirror lines are back where they started, the rotation is the same. But the \(w\) vector points the other way around, it is the negative of where it started. The group element in \(\mathrm{SO}(2)\) is the same \(1\), but in \(\operatorname{Spin}(2)\) the group element is \(-1\).

To get the arrows back to normal, the cat has to go around twice, then the Spin element is back to the identity.

To describe a reflection in 2D, let \(u\) be a unit normal vector to the axis of reflection through the origin. Notice that in the above animation (with 1 mirror only) the cat moves in the direction \(u\) or \(-u\). When \(u\) is more lined up with the starting point the distance it travels is greater. This makes the formula \[v\mapsto v-2(v\cdot u)u\] natural. To check that this makes sense, a reflection should:

  • Leave points on the mirror fixed: if \(v\cdot u=0\), the reflected point is \(v\).
  • Reverse the normal direction: \(u\) maps to \(-u\).
  • Preserve distances and angles, so the reflected cat keeps its shape and size.
  • Return each point to where it started after two reflections.
  • Give the same reflection when the normal \(u\) is replaced by \(-u\).

The formula above satisfies all of these properties, so it is a reflection over the mirror with normal vector u. If the normal vector u has coordinates $u=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)$, then the reflection matrix is \[M(\theta)= \begin{pmatrix} -\cos 2\theta & -\sin 2\theta\\ -\sin 2\theta & \cos 2\theta \end{pmatrix}.\] Combing two reflections leads to the matrix product \[M(\phi)M(\theta) = \begin{pmatrix} -\cos 2\phi & -\sin 2\phi\\ -\sin 2\phi & \cos 2\phi \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} -\cos 2\theta & -\sin 2\theta\\ -\sin 2\theta & \cos 2\theta \end{pmatrix}\] which after applying the double angle identity leads to \[M(\phi)M(\theta)= \begin{pmatrix} \cos\bigl(2(\phi-\theta)\bigr) & -\sin\bigl(2(\phi-\theta)\bigr)\\ \sin\bigl(2(\phi-\theta)\bigr) & \cos\bigl(2(\phi-\theta)\bigr) \end{pmatrix}. \] This is a rotation with angle $2(\phi-\theta)$, which is twice the angle between the two axes of reflection. This angle doubling is illustrated in the next section on loop of rotations. As a preview for the next section, another, compact way to represent a rotation is as $e^{2i(\phi-\theta)}$. Set $\delta=(\phi-\theta)$, another way to represent this rotation $M(\phi)M(\theta)$ is with the complex number $e^{2i\delta}$. These matrices $e^{2i\delta}$ are $SO(2)$. The $\mathrm{Spin}(2)$ representation of this rotation is with $e^{i\delta}$. The map from $\mathrm{Spin}(2)$ to $SO(2)$ squares the complex number, which means $e^{i\delta}$ and $-e^{i\delta}$ produce the same rotation $e^{2i\delta}$. In the cat animation, \(e^{i\delta}\) describes the direction of the second normal arrow relative to the first. Moving the $\mathrm{Spin}(2)$ element in a loop leads to a rotation of $4\pi$, so the cat goes around twice.
