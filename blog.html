Vector Calculus

A vector field $F$ on $\R^n$ assigns to each point $\mathbf x\in \R^n$, a vector $F(\mathbf x)\in \R^n$. For a a point $\mathbf x$ the divergence of $F$ is a number that tells us how much the vector field is pointing out away from $\mathbf x$. If we the region of radius $r$ around around a point $\mathbf x$ is $D_r$, the divergence is \[ \operatorname{div}F(\mathbf x)=\;\lim_{r\to 0}\;\frac{1}{\operatorname{area}(D_r)}\oint_{\partial D_r} F\cdot\mathbf n\,ds .\] The integral on the right side is measuring the flow of $F$ out of the region, and the dividing by the area accounts for the shrinking of the region. Te definition above is informative, but the definition that is easier to work with is \[ \operatorname{div} F = \nabla \cdot F = \sum_{i=1}^n \frac{\partial F^i}{\partial x^i}. \] where \[ \nabla = \left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x^1}, \dots, \frac{\partial}{\partial x^n}\right). \] In summary, the divergence of a vector field in $\R^n$ assigns to each point in $\R^n$ a number which measures the inward/outward flow of the vector field at that point. More flow out is positive, more flow in is negative, which you can see below for $\R^2$.

Divergence of a vector field assigns a scalar to each point. For vector fields $F$ on $\R^3$, there is an operation called $\operatorname{curl}$ which creates another vector field. It measures how much the vector field $F$ is curling, and assigns a vector orthogonal to the curling, according to the right hand rule. The curl integral definition is similar to the divergence definition, the curl in the direction $\mathbf n$ measures how much $F$ is curling in a circle around in the plane orthogonal to $\mathbf n$, \[ (\operatorname{curl} F)(\mathbf x)\cdot\mathbf n \;=\; \lim_{r\to 0}\;\frac{1}{\operatorname{area}(D_r)}\oint_{\partial D_r(\mathbf x,\mathbf n)} F\cdot\mathbf t\,ds . \] The dot product with $\mathbf t$ measures the tangent part of $F$ around the circle $\partial D_r$, so this integral is larger when more of $F$ is curling around the circle.

In this 2D slice, the curl points up out of the screen for counterclockwise rotation and into the screen for clockwise rotation. The faster the wheel spins, the more the vector field is curling. Move the wheel around in the different fields to see the curl of different vector fields.

math interactive Reflections and Rotations

This post is about the math of rotations. The 2D warmup interactively demonstrates how composing an even number of reflections creates rotations and how angles double when you do this. The 3D section is similar but in 3D, the key is that after picking a 2D rotation plane within $\R^3$ we just perform a 2D rotation. After this I include a little bit of the math behind this all, and how the animations are generated.

2D

Reflections in 2D provide a simple starting point for this post. In two dimensions, a mirror is a line called the \(\textit{axis of reflection}\). An object on one side of the axis gets flipped to the other side. A vector But how do we go from reflections to rotations? In the simulation below, you can see how two reflections combine to form a single rotation.

Rotating with mirrors

The mirrors are described by their normal vectors: arrows at right angles to the mirrors. Move the mirrors around by moving the normal vectors, and notice how changing the angle between them changes the rotation. Two things to look out for: first, when the normal vectors are flipped, the mirrors stay the same. Second, the rotation angle is twice the angle between the mirrors.

Loops of rotations

Move the cat around by moving one of the normal vectors. When the cat travels around a full 360 degrees, the normal normal vector is flipped. To get the normal vector back to where it started, the cat goes around twice.

Math details

To describe a reflection in 2D, let \(u\) be a unit normal vector to the reflection mirror. Notice that in the first animation (with 1 mirror only) the cat moves in the direction \(u\) or \(-u\). When \(u\) is more lined up with the starting point the distance it travels is greater. This makes the formula \[v\mapsto v-2(v\cdot u)u\] make sense. Some properties a reflection should satisfy are:

  • Leave points on the mirror fixed: if \(v\cdot u=0\), the reflected point is \(v\).
  • Reverse the normal direction: \(u\) maps to \(-u\).
  • Preserve distances and angles, so the reflected cat keeps its shape and size.
  • Return each point to where it started after two reflections.
  • Give the same reflection when the normal \(u\) is replaced by \(-u\).

The formula above satisfies all of these properties, so it is a reflection over the mirror with normal vector u. If the normal vector u has coordinates $u=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)$, then the reflection matrix is \[M(\theta)= \begin{pmatrix} -\cos 2\theta & -\sin 2\theta\\ -\sin 2\theta & \cos 2\theta \end{pmatrix}.\] Combing two reflections leads to the matrix product \[M(\phi)M(\theta) = \begin{pmatrix} -\cos 2\phi & -\sin 2\phi\\ -\sin 2\phi & \cos 2\phi \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} -\cos 2\theta & -\sin 2\theta\\ -\sin 2\theta & \cos 2\theta \end{pmatrix}\] which after applying the double angle identity leads to \[M(\phi)M(\theta)= \begin{pmatrix} \cos\bigl(2(\phi-\theta)\bigr) & -\sin\bigl(2(\phi-\theta)\bigr)\\ \sin\bigl(2(\phi-\theta)\bigr) & \cos\bigl(2(\phi-\theta)\bigr) \end{pmatrix}. \] This is a rotation with angle $2(\phi-\theta)$, which is twice the angle between the two axes of reflection. This angle doubling is illustrated in the next section on loop of rotations.

3D

This section extends the 2D reflections and rotations to 3D. First off, see how normal vectors create 2D hyperplanes in 3D. Reflecting over two of these creates a rotation, and the angle is doubled, just like in 2D.

Mirrors in 3D
Rotations in 3D

This is a rotation around the z axis. We can represent this rotation a a 3x3 matrix with real entries in $SO(3)$, or as a $2\times 2$ complex matrix in $SU(2)=\operatorname{Spin}(3)$. Similar to the mirror situation in 2D, when we increase $\theta$ by $2\pi$ the $SO(3)$ element is back to where it started, but the $\operatorname{Spin}(3)$ element has to go around twice.

More Math Details

In 2D, we can describe a rotation of $\theta$ as \[R(\theta)=\begin{pmatrix} \cos \theta & -\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{pmatrix}.\] Another way to write this is with complex numbers \[e^{i\theta}=\cos\theta + i\sin\theta,\] so for any vector $\begin{pmatrix} a \\ b\end{pmatrix}\in \R^2$ the image under $R(\theta)$ is the same as the complex number \[e^{i\theta}(a+bi).\] This makes it easy to represent rotations in 2D as complex numbers.
